# Therapeutic framings of the Feelings Wheel words

This file gives a warm, therapy-oriented gloss for each of the 126 feeling words in the
Interactive Feelings Wheel. The goal is to help someone **name and understand** what they
feel — validating, non-clinical, emotionally intelligent language, the way a good therapist
or a CBT/DBT emotion-vocabulary worksheet would gently explain it — not dry dictionary text
and not diagnostic labels.

A later reconciliation step will draw from this alongside plain-dictionary sources; where the
**therapeutic sense diverges from the everyday dictionary sense**, that is flagged in the
"Disambiguation note" column and collected in the [Disambiguations that matter](#disambiguations-that-matter)
section — these matter a great deal for a therapy tool.

**Framework and sources drawn on**
- **Gloria Willcox, "The Feeling Wheel" (1982, *Transactional Analysis Journal* 6(4):274–276)** — origin of this 7-core → secondary → tertiary structure; the wheel is designed to move a person from a vague broad feeling toward a precise, specific one.
- **Robert Plutchik's wheel of emotions** — emotions as adaptive signals arranged by intensity and opposition (e.g. annoyance → anger → rage).
- **Paul Ekman, basic emotions & the *Atlas of Emotions* (atlasofemotions.org)** — each emotion as a "continent" with triggers, graded states, and constructive/destructive actions; the idea that each emotion carries a signal.
- **CBT / DBT emotion-regulation resources** — DBT "emotion vocabulary" and "describing emotions" skills; feelings-list worksheets (e.g. Therapist Aid). Core idea: labeling an emotion accurately ("name it to tame it") is itself regulating, and every emotion has a *function* — it tells you something about your needs, boundaries, or situation.
- **Reputable psychology explainers** — APA Dictionary of Psychology, Greater Good Science Center, verywellmind, PositivePsychology.com Feelings Wheel articles — consulted for meaning and framing; all glosses below are written in my own words.

A recurring therapeutic stance below: **no feeling is "wrong."** Each is information. Naming it
is the first step to working with it.

## Core feelings

The 7 hubs of the wheel. In this model these are broad *feeling-states*, deliberately wider
than any single dictionary definition — an umbrella you sort finer feelings under.

| Word | Therapeutic gloss (≤30 words) | Often signals / arises from | Disambiguation note | Source(s) |
|------|-----------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Angry | A hot, energizing response when something feels unfair, blocked, or crossed. Not "bad" — it flags a boundary or need. | A violated boundary, injustice, blocked goal, or unmet need. | — | Willcox 1982; Ekman/Atlas; Plutchik |
| Disgusted | A recoiling "get this away from me" reaction — toward something (or someone, or an act) that feels wrong, tainted, or repellent. | Something that violates your values or feels contaminating, physically or morally. | Extends beyond food/germs to *moral* disgust (values violated). | Ekman/Atlas; APA |
| Sad | A heavy, slowed-down feeling of loss or lack. It asks you to pause, grieve, and often to reach for comfort or connection. | Loss, absence, disappointment, or unmet longing. | — | Willcox 1982; Ekman/Atlas |
| Happy | A warm, open, expansive good feeling — contentment, joy, and everything bright in between. | Needs met, connection, meaning, safety, play, or accomplishment. | Umbrella state, broader than momentary "cheerful." | Willcox 1982; Plutchik (joy) |
| Surprised | A sudden jolt when reality doesn't match what you expected — a brief, wide-open pause before you know how to feel. | The unexpected: an event, news, or shift that breaks your prediction. | Neutral at its core — can tip pleasant or unpleasant. | Ekman/Atlas; Plutchik |
| Bad | A general "something's off / I don't feel good" state — low, uneasy, or drained — before you've pinned down exactly what. | Depletion, stress, or unmet needs you haven't yet named. | **Not the moral sense ("a bad person").** Here it's a broad *unwell/off* feeling-state — an on-ramp to finer words. | Willcox 1982 |
| Fearful | An alert, protective response to threat or danger — real or anticipated. It readies you to get safe. | Perceived threat, uncertainty, or vulnerability. | — | Willcox 1982; Ekman/Atlas |

## Disambiguations that matter

Words whose **therapeutic / wheel sense differs from (or is easily confused with) the plain
dictionary sense** — worth surfacing explicitly in a therapy tool so users aren't misled:

- **Aroused** — In this wheel's playful branch of *Happy*, it means emotionally **stirred, awakened, animated, lively** — **NOT sexual arousal.** This is the single most important disambiguation for a therapy app.
- **Bad** (core) — A broad **feeling-state** ("I feel bad / off / unwell / drained"), an umbrella to sort finer feelings under. **NOT the moral sense** of being "a bad person."
- **Mad** (secondary) — Means **angry / worked up**, the casual synonym for anger — **NOT "insane" or mentally ill.**
- **Cheeky** — **Playful, mischievous, teasing in good fun** under *Happy* — **NOT rude, insulting, or disrespectful.**
- **Depressed** — Used here as a **feeling-word** (low, heavy, flat), **not a clinical diagnosis** of major depressive disorder.
- **Guilt vs. Shame** — **Guilty / Remorseful** = about a *behavior* ("I did something wrong"). **Ashamed** = about the *self* ("I am wrong / not enough"). Keeping these separate is central in CBT/DBT.
- **Jealous vs. Envy** — Here **Jealous** is *relational*: fear of losing someone/something to a rival — distinct from envy (wanting what another has).
- **Numb** — The **absence** of feeling *is* the feeling: an overwhelm/shutdown protective response, not simply "fine."
- **Nauseated** — Emotional **revulsion/disgust** (which may feel physical), not merely stomach illness.
- **Detestable** — Points **outward** at the object of disgust ("I find this detestable"), not a self-description.
- **Critical** — A **fault-finding / judgmental** stance, not "critically important/urgent."
- **Awful** — Feeling **awful (bad/distressed)**, not describing awful *conduct*.
- **Surprised / Shocked / Startled** — **Valence-neutral at core**: can tip pleasant or unpleasant depending on what follows.
- **Vulnerable / Sensitive** — Framed **neutrally-to-positively** in therapy (openness, attunement, courage), not as weakness or being "too much."
- **Out of Control** — A felt **loss of control** over a situation or emotions, not "misbehaving."
- **Busy** — Here a **felt, pressured over-fullness**, not a neutral report of a full calendar.
- **Distant / Intimate** — **Emotional** distance and **emotional** closeness respectively (Intimate is not necessarily sexual).
- **Courageous** — Bravery **with** fear present, not the absence of fear.
- **Powerful / Proud / Confident** — Healthy **agency and self-regard**, explicitly not domination or arrogance.

Duplicate words defined once (appear at two wheel levels): **Disappointed, Inferior,
Overwhelmed, Embarrassed.**

## Gaps / uncertain

None. All 126 unique words are covered with a confident therapeutic framing.

A few notes on lower-confidence calls (framed, not skipped):
- **Busy** — its placement/sense in the wheel is a bit unusual; framed as a felt over-fullness/overwhelm rather than a neutral schedule report. Reconciliation may want to confirm branch placement.
- **Aroused / Cheeky** — framed strictly per the wheel's playful-*Happy* context; flagged above because the everyday senses are the ones users will assume.
- **Detestable / Nauseated / Repelled / Revolted** — several sit close together under Disgust; glosses aim to differentiate intensity (Repelled → Revolted → Nauseated) and target (Detestable points at the object).
